# Creating Logical Flow When Writing Scientific Articles
**Authors**: Edward Barroga and Glafera Janet Matanguihan | **Pages**: 14 | **Modules**: 5 | **Generated**: 2026-09-17

## How to Use This Skill

- With a draft, diagnose the highest-level flow break first, then revise only what the evidence supports.
- With a section name (`introduction`, `methods`, `results`, or `discussion`), apply the corresponding sequence in ch05.
- With a concept (`topic sentence`, `transition`, `coherence`), consult the topic index and load the relevant chapter.
- With `ch01`–`ch05`, load that module for deeper guidance and examples.

Do not treat surface transitions as a cure for missing reasoning. Establish the ideas and their order first; add transitions only after the argument is sound.

## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### Diagnose flow at three nested levels

Use the **article → paragraph → sentence** hierarchy when a draft feels disjointed. First verify that the article tells one sequential story tied to its purpose. Then verify that each paragraph develops one main idea. Only then repair sentence wording. Local polish cannot repair a missing argument or a misplaced section.

### Build before linking

Use this sequence for revision:

1. State the thesis, purpose, or section-level claim.
2. Arrange supporting ideas in a simple, defensible order.
3. Build paragraphs around one topic sentence and relevant evidence.
4. Connect sentences and paragraphs with transitions that name the real relationship.
5. Test whether the conclusion follows from the presented evidence.

Transitions expose relationships; they do not create them. If “therefore” joins claims without causal support, revise the reasoning rather than the connector.

### Use the three-part paragraph

Construct each paragraph as:

- **First/topic sentence** — makes a specific assertion and frames the paragraph.
- **Middle/supporting sentences** — explain, define, qualify, or provide evidence for that assertion.
- **Last/concluding sentence** — synthesizes the point and, when useful, prepares the next paragraph.

Apply the **one idea–one paragraph** principle. Make the theme of one sentence a starting point for the next when linear continuity helps the reader.

### Separate logical and lexical coherence

- **Logical coherence** comes from orderly placement of claims and supporting details.
- **Lexical coherence** comes from deliberate wording that helps readers track the same idea: repeat a key term when precision matters, use a clear pronoun when its referent is unmistakable, or refer back to the previous paragraph before introducing the next topic.

Repair logical coherence before varying vocabulary. Synonyms can obscure a technical concept that should remain stable.

### Select transitions by relationship

Choose a transition only after naming the relationship:

- **Additive** — adds, clarifies, illustrates, or emphasizes.
- **Adversative** — contrasts, concedes, distinguishes, or offers an alternative.
- **Causal** — marks reason, condition, purpose, effect, or consequence.
- **Sequential** — orders stages, resumes a thread, summarizes, or concludes.

Scale the device to the gap: a word can connect nearby clauses, a transitional sentence can bridge paragraphs, and a transitional paragraph can orient the reader to a new major section.

### Preserve the article’s internal logic across IMRAD

Use the study purpose as the invariant thread:

- **Introduction**: problem → current knowledge and limitation → aim and contribution.
- **Methods**: design → setting → subjects → data collection → analysis.
- **Results**: figures/tables reveal the evidence → report main outcomes in a deliberate sequence.
- **Discussion**: purpose and key findings → interpret findings in the same order → limitations and strengths → implications, conclusions, and remaining gaps.

Readers should be able to trace each aim to a method, each method to a result, and each key result to its interpretation.

### Prefer precision and brevity, but preserve meaning

Replace inflated phrases with direct equivalents (`in order to` → `to`; `a large number of` → `many`) and remove empty modifiers or clauses. Use short declarative sentences to emphasize key content; the paper suggests roughly 20–25 words as a practical target, not an inflexible cap. Keep necessary qualifications, methodological detail, and uncertainty.
