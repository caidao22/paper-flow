# Chapter 4: Transitions and Coherence

## Core Idea

Transitions make an existing relationship visible. Coherence arises from both the logical order of ideas and the lexical cues that let readers track those ideas.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Transition scale**: word/phrase → sentence → paragraph.
  - When to use: Choose the smallest device that spans the conceptual gap.
  - How: Use a word for a local relationship, a sentence to connect two paragraph topics, or a short orienting paragraph before a major section shift.
- **Four relationship types**: additive, adversative, causal, sequential.
  - When to use: Any explicit connective.
  - How: State the relationship in plain language first (“B contrasts with A”); then choose a connector whose meaning matches it.
  - Failure mode: An impressive connector can overstate an uncertain or merely adjacent relationship.
- **Dual coherence audit**: logical coherence plus lexical coherence.
  - When to use: A passage is factually ordered but still difficult to track, or linguistically smooth but logically weak.
  - How: First outline claims and support; then inspect term consistency, referents, keyword repetition, and backward links.

## Key Concepts

- **Additive transition**: Adds, illustrates, clarifies, emphasizes, or marks similarity.
- **Adversative transition**: Contrasts, concedes, distinguishes, dismisses, or presents an alternative.
- **Causal transition**: Expresses cause, condition, purpose, consequence, or result.
- **Sequential transition**: Orders stages, continues a process, resumes a topic, summarizes, or concludes.
- **Transitional sentence**: Refers to the previous topic while establishing the next topic.
- **Transitional paragraph**: Orients readers to a new major section or information block.
- **Lexical coherence**: Continuity created by consistent, navigational word choice.

## Mental Models

- Treat every transition as a semantic claim that must be true.
- Use a bridge sentence with two anchors: one backward-looking term and one forward-looking term.
- Prefer no connector over a misleading connector; adjacency can be sufficient when order is obvious.

## Anti-patterns

- **Connector substitution**: Using “therefore” where the evidence establishes only sequence or correlation.
- **Transition saturation**: Adding a connector to nearly every sentence, making prose mechanical.
- **Floating pronoun**: “This” or “these” lacks a clear noun referent.
- **False variety**: Replacing the same construct with different words, weakening lexical coherence.
- **Section teleportation**: Starting a new major topic without telling readers why it follows.

## Worked Example

Weak bridge: “Model accuracy increased. However, inference time decreased.” The connector signals contrast, yet both changes may be desirable and jointly caused by the same modification.

Revision depends on the intended relation:

- Addition: “Model accuracy increased; in addition, inference time decreased.”
- Unexpected co-benefit: “Despite the usual accuracy–efficiency trade-off, accuracy increased while inference time decreased.”
- Cause, only if demonstrated: “Because pruning removed redundant operations, inference time decreased.”

Choose after establishing what the evidence warrants.

## Key Takeaways

1. Name the relationship before selecting the transition.
2. Use words for local links, sentences for paragraph bridges, and paragraphs for major shifts.
3. Audit idea order separately from vocabulary and referents.
4. Repeat a technical keyword when it protects identity and precision.
5. Never let a connector strengthen a claim beyond its evidence.
