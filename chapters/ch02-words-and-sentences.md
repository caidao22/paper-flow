# Chapter 2: Precise Words and Clear Sentences

## Core Idea

Sentence-level flow begins with words that carry exact meaning and sentences organized around one topic. Concision removes friction but must not remove scientific content.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Precision-first concision**: Prefer the briefest wording that preserves the intended meaning.
  - When to use: A sentence is inflated, indirect, or slow to parse.
  - How: Identify the indispensable claim and qualifiers; replace wordy phrases; remove redundant modifiers and nominalizations; reread for altered meaning.
  - Failure mode: Blind shortening can delete uncertainty, boundary conditions, or methodological detail.
- **One-topic sentence**: Give each sentence one controlling subject–action relation.
  - When to use: A sentence contains several embedded clauses or forces rereading.
  - How: Find the main actor and action, move them near each other, split secondary claims, and keep modifiers beside what they modify.
- **Lexical linking**: Connect adjacent sentences with stable terms, clear pronouns, or purposeful repetition.
  - When to use: Sentences are grammatical but feel disconnected.
  - How: End one sentence on a concept that the next sentence takes up, or repeat the key technical term when synonymy would blur identity.

## Key Concepts

- **Nominalization**: A noun construction that can sometimes be expressed more directly as a verb.
- **Modifier**: A word or phrase that qualifies another element; excessive or misplaced modifiers impede clarity.
- **Parallel structure**: Matching grammatical forms for comparable ideas.
- **Collocation**: A conventional combination of words in a discipline or language.
- **Declarative sentence**: A direct statement useful for emphasizing a key finding or claim.

## Mental Models

- Use “meaning per word” when trimming: remove cost, not content.
- Think of the subject and main verb as the sentence spine; keep them visible.
- Use sentence-length variation deliberately, with short sentences for emphasis rather than as a mechanical rule.

## Anti-patterns

- **Inflated phrases**: “in order to,” “a large number of,” or “are indicative of” when “to,” “many,” or “indicate” is exact.
- **Empty intensifiers**: “very,” “basically,” or “generally” without a defined scientific function.
- **Clause nesting**: Multiple `which`, `that`, `although`, `where`, or `when` clauses interrupt the main claim.
- **Thesaurus drift**: Changing technical terms merely to avoid repetition.
- **Mechanical length caps**: Cutting a necessary sentence only because it exceeds a target.

## Worked Example

Original pattern: “The histological types were distributed and classified as adenocarcinoma in 51 cases, squamous cell carcinoma in 24 cases, large cell carcinoma in 15 cases, and carcinoid in 10 cases.”

Revised structure: “The histological types were adenocarcinoma (n = 51), squamous cell carcinoma (n = 24), large cell carcinoma (n = 15), and carcinoid (n = 10).”

The revision removes two weak verbs and expresses counts compactly while preserving every category and value. Apply the same operation only after listing the facts that must survive.

## Key Takeaways

1. Choose discipline-specific, formal, precise terms.
2. Keep one principal topic per sentence and the subject close to its action.
3. Combine genuinely parallel ideas; split competing claims.
4. Use roughly 20–25 words as a warning threshold, not a law.
5. After shortening, verify qualifiers, quantities, and causal strength.
