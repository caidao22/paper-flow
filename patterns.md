# Patterns

## Top-Down Flow Audit
**When to use**: A manuscript is hard to follow and local editing has not helped.
**How**: Summarize the article’s claim; map purpose → method → evidence → interpretation → conclusion; label every paragraph; inspect sentence links only after structural gaps are fixed.
**Trade-offs**: Requires more restructuring than line editing, but avoids polishing material that must move or disappear.

## One Idea–One Paragraph
**When to use**: A paragraph contains multiple findings, topics, or rhetorical jobs.
**How**: Write one controlling assertion; retain only its evidence and explanation; move independent claims to separate paragraphs; synthesize or bridge at the end.
**Trade-offs**: Too-rigid use can produce choppy micro-paragraphs; related details should remain together.

## Three-Part Paragraph
**When to use**: Drafting an evidence-bearing paragraph.
**How**: Open with a specific topic sentence, develop it with evidence and explanation, and close by synthesizing or preparing the next idea.
**Trade-offs**: The concluding sentence is optional when the paragraph’s resolution is already unmistakable.

## Given-to-New Progression
**When to use**: Relevant sentences still read like a list.
**How**: Begin each new sentence from a concept established in the previous context, then add new information; repeat precise keywords or use unambiguous references.
**Trade-offs**: Mechanical repetition can sound heavy; prioritize traceability over superficial variety.

## Precision-First Compression
**When to use**: Prose is inflated or indirect.
**How**: Record the claim, qualifiers, quantities, and scope; replace wordy phrases; activate strong verbs; remove empty modifiers; compare the revision against the recorded facts.
**Trade-offs**: Excessive compression can erase uncertainty or necessary methodological detail.

## Semantic Transition Selection
**When to use**: Connecting clauses, sentences, or paragraphs.
**How**: Name the intended relationship; select additive, adversative, causal, or sequential language; verify that the evidence warrants that relation; scale the transition to the gap.
**Trade-offs**: Explicit connectors improve navigation but become distracting when the order is already obvious.

## Two-Anchor Paragraph Bridge
**When to use**: Two coherent paragraphs do not connect smoothly.
**How**: In one sentence, refer to the completed topic and state how it motivates, limits, contrasts with, or enables the next topic.
**Trade-offs**: A bridge cannot rescue two unrelated paragraphs; reorder or remove content first.

## IMRAD Alignment Grid
**When to use**: Planning or auditing an empirical paper.
**How**: Create one row per aim or research question and columns for method, result, discussion, and conclusion. Fill each cell with the corresponding element and repair empty, duplicated, or unsupported cells.
**Trade-offs**: Complex exploratory studies may require many-to-many links; document them rather than forcing a false one-to-one mapping.

## Visual-First Results Drafting
**When to use**: Analyses are complete but the Results narrative is unclear.
**How**: Draft figures for trends, tables for exact values, select those tied to objectives, summarize each in one sentence, then order the summaries into a results story.
**Trade-offs**: Visual order should follow the scientific question, not software output order.

## Ordered Discussion Arc
**When to use**: A Discussion repeats results or wanders through literature.
**How**: Restate purpose and key findings; interpret findings in Results order; compare each with literature and explain relevance; state limitations and strengths; close with bounded implications and gaps.
**Trade-offs**: Four to six paragraphs is a useful model, not a requirement; adapt to journal and study complexity.
